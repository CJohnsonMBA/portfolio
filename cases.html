Building an alliance practice from zero in 90 days

Data and AI consultancy · Four cloud data platforms

The situation. The company had strong technical skills and had sold platform work for years, but it had no formal alliances. I was brought in as the alliance leader for four platforms, with a mandate to decide where to focus.

The decision. I ranked the four platforms the way I'd ranked partners before: on incentives, rebates, market development funds, partner support and services revenue. One hyperscaler came out clearly on top, with the most comprehensive program, the highest rebates and funding, and the most support. The second hyperscaler's program didn't justify pursuing. The two data platforms became tier 2. Because we work with customers of both, our events promote data lakehouse technology without naming either vendor, so we don't alienate anyone.

What I found. About $20M in platform business had closed since 2022, and none of it had been registered with the partner. By the time I arrived, none of it could still qualify. That was money left on the table.

Changing behavior. Sales had been told to register deals several times, with no result. When a seller in Canada asked for access to a customer funding voucher, I asked him to register his deal first. Once it was done, I showed the team everything we could become eligible for. The pushback stopped.

Making it easy. I built a custom Claude skill loaded with the partner's deal registration rules and submission deadlines. It pulls CRM data daily, tells each seller which registration method applies to each deal, and shows a countdown until a deal is no longer eligible. Sellers only mark each deal complete, in progress or upcoming.

Result. Within two weeks of registering deals, we crossed a program threshold that made us eligible for rebates in one solution area. Joint go-to-market work followed: events, webinars, account co-selling and introductions between the two teams. All of this happened within my first 90 days.

The lesson. Unregistered deals are invisible revenue. People change habits when the right step is easy and they can see what's in it for them.

Building the onboarding engine for a new product's partner channel

Global enterprise technology company · Partner manager · 300+ partners

The situation. As a new partner manager covering more than 300 partners, I was also asked to grow sales of our risk and compliance platform through partners. As each new module launched, more companies wanted to sell it. Every one had to be onboarded and certified on highly technical software, and I was still learning the job.

The decision. Instead of handling partners one at a time, I built a system. I focused on the partners most ready to sell (technically capable, certified or close, and eager to move forward) and supported the rest by email.

What I did. I set up a phased handoff. Our product lead engaged each partner's technical team first, then handed off to me for distributor signup, certifications, MDF and deal registration. I built standard decks and a tracker that followed about 65 priority partners across five regions through six stages: accepted invitation, joint business growth plan, monthly meetings, working opportunities, co-marketing, and live in the CRM with deal registration. It also tracked certifications, distributors, partner levels and open issues.

Finding out why a partner program was drifting

Hyperscaler partner program · North America and Asia-Pacific

The situation. I joined after the program had launched in Asia-Pacific, to run it in North America and compare results across the two regions. The program dashboard showed partner sellers spending less time in the partner portal. For some, time had dropped by 40% or more.

What I did. I went past the regular check-ins and interviewed sellers in depth. After talking with about half of them, the pattern was clear: they weren't covering the accounts, because the accounts didn't match their region or vertical. I reported the findings alongside the Asia-Pacific results so leadership could compare how the regions operated.

The hard call. The engineers sat with the Asia-Pacific team, and new features went out as soon as someone had the idea. I pushed for a different rule: don't release a feature until customers confirm they need it. For example, the team built marketing decks from customer insights, but customers were still learning the core product and hadn't asked for them.

Result. Thousands of accounts were realigned to the right sellers, and portal usage rose 40% within two months. The sellers were grateful. We also stopped releasing new features in the US and focused on the core technology.

The lesson. When engagement drops, ask before you build. The cause is often a coverage problem, not a product gap.

Turning a sister division into a partner

Global enterprise technology company · Consulting division · Enterprise accounts

The situation. While reviewing my accounts after a CRM update, I found eight deals that stood out for their size, some over $15M. Our consulting division was selling AI into my accounts, and I wasn't involved.

The decision. Leadership was credited on this technology no matter who closed it, so there was little internal reason to step in. I went directly to the consulting lead on one of the deals instead of escalating.

What I did. I learned his plan: he was proposing an AI search and discovery solution alongside other AI tools, and he would need to build and bill for a custom demo environment. I had access to resources his team didn't: free demos, sandbox environments, and a design thinking team that runs onsite workshops and builds the customer's business case at no cost. I offered him all of it free, in exchange for routing the software licenses through my partner.

Result. Everyone came out ahead. Consulting got free services that strengthened its pitch, my partner earned the license revenue, which gave me leverage with them, and I closed $200K+ in software over a full-year sales cycle. I was setting up the same approach in larger accounts when a reorganization ended the effort.

The lesson. Internal divisions are partners too. Find what you can give that they can't get on their own, and structure the deal so every side wins.

Making leads something partners earn

Global enterprise technology company · Partner ecosystem

The situation. Partners expected us to hand them leads. We had just added a marketing signal to our CRM that surfaced live, qualified prospects, so I suddenly had valuable leads to give out.

The decision. I gave leads as a reward for effort that brought in business. A partner who brought me into a deal might get two leads. Account planning, surfacing opportunities and joining events also earned them. I kept the rule within my team and didn't announce it to partners, because I didn't want them working only when leads were on offer. I made exceptions for new partners when I believed they would truly reach out and work the leads.

Result. Partners became more engaged, and my team adopted the approach. It also built trust: partners saw that I wanted to help them grow, not just hand out names.

The lesson. Reward the work, not the ask. Leads should reinforce a partner's effort, not replace it.
